About this picture — what is real and what is drawn for clarity

The geography

The lake, Antelope Island, the Promontory peninsula, the Wasatch range and the three rivers that feed the lake — Bear from the north, Weber from the east, Jordan from the south out of Utah Lake — sit where they sit in the basin. Everything is squared onto a grid and scaled down, so distances are approximate and the mountains are far steeper than life.

The two arms, and why one is pink

The Union Pacific causeway has split the lake since 1959. Gunnison Bay, north of it, receives almost no river water, sits near salt saturation at roughly 28–30% and is coloured by halophilic archaea and Dunaliella salina — the rose you can see from a plane. Gilbert Bay, south of it, takes the Bear, Weber and Jordan and stays blue-green at roughly half that salinity. Here the north arm is always rose and the south arm reddens as the lake falls, because that is the direction salinity moves; it is an illustration of the salinity the model tracks, not a separate salinity model.

The water

Lake depth is the model's own elevation for the chosen year, mapped onto the block grid between 4,186 ft and the top of the healthy band. A block of water is about three feet of lake. The surface area grows as it deepens, which is why evaporation climbs with it.

The streams

Each lever's water enters on the river it actually comes from: rights, the mandate, the charge and crop switching are farm water, so they come down the Bear and the Weber, where the basin's hay ground is. The lawn restriction is city water, so it comes down the Jordan. Phragmites removal happens in the deltas, so it enters at the shoreline. Cloud seeding is the only lever that falls as snow. Stream thickness is proportional to acre-feet.

Evaporation and snowfall

Snowfall is drawn at a fixed rate over the range, lifted by whatever cloud seeding is buying. Evaporation is the model's own figure for the lake at that level, drawn as vapour leaving the surface. Neither is a simulation of weather; both are the model's annual numbers shown as motion.

    • ΔE(ΔQ) lake response from the USU simulations: about 0.011 ft per KAF for the first 250 KAF, 0.0076 ft per KAF after (diminishing as the lake's surface area and evaporation grow).
    • λ avoided damage per foot: the $1.7–2.2B/yr economic loss at risk spread over the 6.9 ft gap between today and 4,198 ft.
    • pi − mi farmer surplus on a lease: payment minus the net value of water in hay (≈$116–150/AF). Leases at $236–650/AF leave farmers better off, which is why leasing ranks first on welfare, not just cost.
